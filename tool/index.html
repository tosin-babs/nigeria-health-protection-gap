§ 2

Determining the premium

The price begins with what households already spend out of pocket. Care the package excludes comes off. Then care people would use once it costs them almost nothing goes on — the single largest adjustment in the calculation. What the member still pays at the counter comes off again, leaving the claims cost. Running costs and margins take it to the price.

§ 3

Affordability test

A health premium is conventionally treated as affordable when it stays below 5% of what a person consumes in a year. Households are ranked into five equal groups from poorest to richest and the premium is measured against each group's consumption.

§ 4

Solvency test

Members pay what they can afford; a government subsidy makes up the difference. If the two together fall short of the claims that arrive, the scheme runs out of money — an event actuaries call ruin. The question is how large the subsidy has to be before that becomes unlikely enough.

§ 5

Basis and limitations

What this is. A calculator that runs the model from a working paper measuring Nigeria's health-protection gap and pricing insurance for the informal sector. It uses the paper's own data and the paper's own code path. Leave every assumption at its loaded value and it returns the paper's published figures.

Where the numbers come from. The Nigeria General Household Survey-Panel wave 4 (2018/19), collected by the National Bureau of Statistics with the World Bank. Costs are built from a health module that asks each household member what they sought care for and what they paid, over a four-week window for outpatient care and twelve months for hospital admissions. Scaling that four-week window to a year is the largest single source of uncertainty in the underlying estimates. The page carries the aggregated cost distribution only — never the microdata.

How solvency is worked out. The scheme's surplus each year is Uₜ = Uₜ₋₁ + n(C + s)(1 − e) − Sₜ — opening funds, plus contribution C and subsidy s from each of n members after an expense ratio e, less the year's claims. Ruin is a surplus below zero in any year. The paper draws each pool-year as a multinomial over 750 distinct cost values; that is too slow for a browser, so this page matches the first three moments of the same distribution with a translated gamma over 4,000 paths. The two agree to within 0.7% at the 99th percentile and 1.1% at the 99.9th percentile of annual claims across every pool size and take-up pattern offered here. The required subsidy is found by bisection on a fixed set of draws, so moving an assumption never changes the answer through simulation noise alone.

What it is not. The benefit package is an assumption, not the NHIA's schedule. The demand response is borrowed from a United States experiment conducted in the 1970s. Households that go without care appear as zero spenders, so every figure here understates unmet need. Nothing on this page is advice on what a scheme should charge.

Source. Nigeria General Household Survey-Panel wave 4 (2018/19), National Bureau of Statistics with the World Bank LSMS-ISA program. Microdata are not redistributed.
